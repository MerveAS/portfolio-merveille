Monitoring floods
when the satellite is blind

Merveille Aganze Sami

MEL & Database Management Advisor

There is a cruel irony in flood monitoring across Central Africa: the period when satellite imagery is most needed is precisely the one when none is available. During the rainy season, the Congo Basin disappears under near-permanent cloud cover. Optical sensors — Sentinel-2, Landsat — see nothing. You wait for a clear sky while the water rises, recedes, and the flood is over before the first usable image arrives.

Yet there is a family of sensors for which clouds simply do not exist. Here is how to set up flood monitoring that works when nothing else does — the physical principle, the processing chain, and above all the pitfalls that are usually discovered far too late.

Why radar sees what optical cannot

An optical sensor is passive: it records sunlight reflected by the surface. If a cloud gets in the way, it records the cloud. It is that simple, and that blocking.

A Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) works the other way around: it is active. It emits its own wave and measures what comes back. Sentinel-1, the radar satellite of the European Copernicus programme, transmits in C-band — a wavelength of about 5.6 centimetres. The droplets that make up clouds are hundreds of times smaller than that. At this scale the cloud is simply transparent: the wave passes through on the way down, bounces off the ground, and passes through again on the way back.

Practical consequence: because it generates its own wave, radar also works at night. Two major constraints of optical imagery — cloud cover and solar illumination — disappear at once.

The detection principle: water is black in radar

What makes flood detection so elegant in radar is that open water has an almost caricatural signature. A calm water surface is smooth at the scale of the wave: it behaves like a mirror. The emitted wave travels away from the satellite instead of scattering in all directions. Almost nothing returns to the sensor.

As a result, water appears deep black on a radar image, while vegetation, rough soils and built-up areas send back far more signal and appear light grey. The contrast is so pronounced that a simple threshold on the backscatter coefficient is often enough to separate water from everything else.

A word on polarisation, because it is a choice that matters. Sentinel-1 generally acquires in dual polarisation, VV and VH. For open water, VV polarisation usually offers the best contrast and remains the default choice. VH, more sensitive to canopy structure, becomes useful when looking for water under vegetation — a considerably harder case.

Building an automated chain in five steps

The operational value is not in producing one beautiful map, but in getting an up-to-date map at every satellite revisit, without manual intervention. On Google Earth Engine, where the Sentinel-1 archive is already pre-processed and computation happens server-side, the chain fits in five steps.

  1. 1Build a pre-flood baseline. A median composite of dry-season images gives the "normal" state of the territory: permanent rivers, usual water bodies. Without this reference, a river cannot be distinguished from a flood.
  2. 2Filter the speckle. Every radar image carries a characteristic grainy noise, speckle, inherent to coherent imaging. A spatial filter (Refined Lee, for instance) or a multi-temporal average reduces it substantially — an essential step before any thresholding.
  3. 3Threshold to extract water. A fixed threshold on VV backscatter works, but drifts from one scene to the next. Otsu's method, which automatically determines the optimal threshold from the image's bimodal histogram, gives far more stable results over time.
  4. 4Subtract permanent water. The difference between detected water and the pre-flood baseline isolates new water — the flood itself, the only information an alert system cares about.
  5. 5Cross-reference and publish. The flooded extent is intersected with the layers that give it meaning: settlements, infrastructure, plots, project intervention areas. The output feeds a dashboard, and crossing an area threshold triggers an alert.

Sentinel-1 revisits the same area every six to twelve days depending on latitude and constellation configuration. This is not real time — but compared with waiting for a clear sky that may take weeks, it is a change of order of magnitude. And the data is free and open.

The pitfalls discovered too late

This is the part rarely found in tutorials, and yet the one that determines the operational reliability of a system. Radar sees through clouds, but it is not infallible.

  • Wind roughens water. A choppy water surface stops being a mirror: it returns signal and no longer appears dark enough. The result is an underestimated flood on windy days. This is the main cause of false negatives.
  • Some dry surfaces imitate water. Smooth sand, asphalt and very flat bare soils also return very little signal. Without an additional filter, they are classified as flooded.
  • Water under vegetation or in cities escapes thresholding. Beneath a forest canopy or between buildings, the wave bounces several times (double-bounce effect) and returns stronger, not weaker. Flooding there appears bright — the opposite of what is being looked for. These contexts require dedicated approaches.
  • Terrain creates blind zones. In rugged areas, radar shadow and layover produce artefacts easily mistaken for water.

The most effective safeguard fits in one sentence: filter by topography. A digital elevation model, or better a Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND) index, immediately removes any "water" detected on a steep slope or well above the drainage network. Water does not climb hills: that simple common sense, translated into a spatial rule, eliminates most false positives.

What it changes in practice

For a project team, the benefit is not technical, it is decisional. Having a flood extent map a few days after the event, rather than a few weeks, changes the nature of the questions that can be answered: which settlements are affected, which roads are cut, which plots of an agricultural programme are under water, where to send a verification mission first.

It also changes the relationship with donors and partners. A dated, reproducible and documented flooded-area estimate is worth more than a qualitative report written from memory three months later. And because the chain is automated, the same method applies to the next flood without redoing the work.

Key takeaways

  • C-band radar sees through clouds and works at night: both optical limitations disappear
  • Open water appears very dark in VV polarisation — thresholding is often enough to extract it
  • A pre-flood baseline is essential to separate new water from permanent water
  • Wind, smooth soils, vegetation and terrain are the four error sources to handle explicitly
  • A topographic filter (DEM or HAND) removes most false positives

Where to start

Sentinel-1 data is freely available through the Copernicus Data Space, and already pre-processed in the Google Earth Engine catalogue — which removes the most off-putting step of the radar chain. The most effective way to begin: pick a past, well-documented flood in your own territory, reconstruct the flood extent map, then compare the result with available field reports. It is by measuring your own discrepancies that you learn to calibrate the method — and earn the right to use it in a real situation.

The most useful data is never the most beautiful image. It is the one that arrives when nothing else is visible.
